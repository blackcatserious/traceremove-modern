Whether you are an engineer building AI systems, a policymaker regulating them, or a citizen affected by them, philosophical literacy about AI is no longer optional. The decisions being made right now — about deployment, regulation, rights, and responsibility — rest on philosophical assumptions that most people have never examined.

This reading list is designed to build that philosophical foundation efficiently. I have organized it into three tiers: foundations you should read first, contemporary essentials that address current AI systems, and frontier work that grapples with questions we don't yet know how to answer.

## Tier 1: Philosophical Foundations

**Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950).** The paper that started the field. Turing's "imitation game" — now called the Turing Test — remains the most influential framework for thinking about machine intelligence, and understanding its limitations is essential for evaluating modern AI claims. Available free on numerous academic sites.

**John Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980).** The Chinese Room argument remains the most powerful philosophical objection to the idea that symbol manipulation alone constitutes understanding. Every debate about whether LLMs "understand" language is, at bottom, a debate about Searle's argument.

**Hubert Dreyfus, "What Computers Can't Do" (1972, revised 1992).** Dreyfus drew on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty to argue that human intelligence is fundamentally embodied and situated in ways that digital computation cannot replicate. His arguments, dismissed for decades, now look prescient in light of LLM limitations.

**Daniel Dennett, "Consciousness Explained" (1991).** Dennett's functionalist approach to consciousness provides the strongest philosophical case for the *possibility* of machine consciousness. Whether you agree or not, understanding his framework is essential for engaging with current debates.

## Tier 2: Contemporary Essentials

**Kate Crawford, "Atlas of AI" (2021).** An empirical and philosophical examination of AI as a system of power — from the mines that produce the minerals for chips to the underpaid workers who label training data. Essential for understanding AI as a political and material phenomenon, not just a technical one.

**Arvind Narayanan & Sayash Kapoor, "AI Snake Oil" (2024).** A rigorous analysis of which AI applications work, which don't, and why the public is systematically misled about AI capabilities. The epistemological framework here complements philosophical analysis.

**Shannon Vallor, "Technology and the Virtues" (2016).** Vallor applies virtue ethics to technology, arguing that we need to cultivate specific character traits to flourish alongside powerful technologies. Her work provides an ethical framework that goes beyond rule-based approaches to AI ethics.

**Brian Christian, "The Alignment Problem" (2020).** The most accessible introduction to the technical and philosophical challenges of ensuring AI systems do what we intend. Bridges the gap between machine learning research and philosophical concerns about value alignment.

## Tier 3: Frontier Questions

**David Chalmers, "Reality+" (2022).** Chalmers extends his work on consciousness to virtual reality and simulation, raising questions about whether digital minds could be genuine minds. The implications for AI consciousness are direct and profound.

**Raphaël Millière & Charles Rathkopf, recent work on LLM cognition (2024-2025).** At the cutting edge of philosophy of AI, examining whether transformer architectures implement anything resembling cognitive processes. Follow their work on PhilPapers.

**The growing literature on AI epistemology.** My own work in this area — *Epistemic Risks in AI: Knowledge, Truth, and Uncertainty* — examines the conditions under which AI outputs can count as knowledge. This is one of the fastest-growing subfields in philosophy of AI.

## How to Use This List

If you have limited time, start with Turing and Searle from Tier 1 (both are short papers), then read Crawford and Narayanan from Tier 2. These four texts will give you a philosophical foundation stronger than 95% of people working in AI today.

If you want to go deeper, read them all — but read actively. The point is not to memorize arguments but to develop the ability to think critically about AI claims. When someone says "this AI understands medicine," you should immediately hear Searle's Chinese Room. When someone says "AI will solve everything," you should hear Dreyfus's embodiment critique.

Philosophy is not a luxury in the age of AI. It is a survival skill.
